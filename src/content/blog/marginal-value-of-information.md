In 1971, Yujiro Hayami and Willis Peterson [estimated](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/13711) what one more dollar spent on USDA crop and livestock surveys would return to the public. By their model, each dollar that cut the typical sampling error from 2.5 to 2.0 percent returned more than $600, and each dollar over the next half point, from 2.0 to 1.5 percent, returned $90 to $100. They modeled two ways reporting error does damage: holders of grain misjudge how much to store after a crop report, and livestock producers misjudge how much to raise. The welfare gain is the cost of those mistakes avoided. They concluded the government underinvested in the surveys.

In what I have read since, estimates of that kind, priced per dollar over a stated gain in accuracy, are rare.

The question has a new audience. On August 1, 2025, the President fired the Commissioner of Labor Statistics. In April 2026, Nicholas Bloom, Erica Groshen, Duncan Hobbs and Michael Strain [estimated](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35135) what that cost. An event study of policy uncertainty around the firing, mapped into output, gives a loss of about $20 billion against a BLS budget of $704 million, which they describe as about $25 per dollar. They call it a back-of-the-envelope estimate of "the marginal value of public trust in official statistics." It measures one shock to trust, through one channel, uncertainty, against the whole budget. The census valuations below share that shape, and the shape limits what they can say about the next dollar.

## What the census valuations measure

Statistical offices commission valuations of their censuses, and the results cluster.

| Valuation | Return per dollar, as reported |
|---|---|
| [Stats NZ, 2014](https://www.stats.govt.nz/assets/Research/Valuing-the-Census/valuing-the-census.pdf) | net benefit of about 5 |
| [Stats NZ, 2021](https://www.stats.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/Reports/Value-of-the-New-Zealand-census-August-2021/Value-of-the-New-Zealand-census-August-2021.pdf) | benefit of about 4 |
| [Lateral Economics for the ABS, 2019](https://www.abs.gov.au/census/about-census/value-census) | value of about 6 |
| [ONS, Census 2021 business case](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/populationestimates/articles/assessingthebenefitsofcensus2021englandandwales/2022-09-28) | benefit of about 5, forecast |
| [Deloitte for ONS, 2016](https://www.ons.gov.uk/file?uri=%2Fmethodology%2Fmethodologicalpublications%2Fgeneralmethodology%2Fonsworkingpaperseries%2Fvaluingofficialstatisticswithconjointanalysisapril2021%2Fdeloittemeasuringthevalueofofficialstatistics.areporttoonsfinalapproved.pdf), GDP, inflation and labour market statistics | core user benefits, ratio of 1.7 |

These are aggregate benefit-cost estimates with different conventions. The 2014 New Zealand figure is net of the census's cost, and Deloitte counts core user benefits only. Each rests on assumptions that do much of the work. To assess what the 2021 census for England and Wales delivered, ONS [assumes](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/populationestimates/methodologies/methodsforassessingthebenefitsofcensus2021englandandwales) that funding allocated with census population estimates yields a net welfare gain of 0.015 percent of the money allocated, and 0.053 percent when a formula uses census attributes. Both figures come from departments' earlier modelling of what the next-best data would have misallocated.

The 2011 census shows where percentages that small come from. [Rerunning](https://isi-web.org/sites/default/files/import/files-2015/IPS087-P4-S.pdf) the health funding formula on lists of patients registered with GPs, the next-best data, produced a first-year allocation error of £800 million. A similar test on local government funding gave £125 million. The pot was fixed, so every pound one area gained, another lost. The benefit case counted only the asymmetry: the benefit of receiving a pound more than an area needs is smaller than the harm of receiving a pound less. That modeled net efficiency loss started at about £70 million a year and was assumed to decay as the data aged.

## Why a value of information looks like an MVPF

Nathaniel Hendren and Ben Sprung-Keyser's [marginal value of public funds](https://bsprungkeyser.com/assets/MVPF_Mar_2020_QJE.pdf) divides beneficiaries' willingness to pay for a policy by its net cost to the government, long-run fiscal effects included. They computed it for 133 policies and describe it as "the price faced by the government to redistribute across beneficiaries of different types of policies."

Nothing in that definition restricts the policy to a transfer. An expansion of the American Community Survey can be evaluated the same way: willingness to pay for the extra information, divided by its net fiscal cost. A benefit-cost ratio on survey spending is a different object, and so is a decision theorist's value of information. The MVPF needs beneficiaries' willingness to pay on top and net fiscal cost underneath, and three things make it hard to fill in.

**The numerator is a decision problem.** For an unconditional cash transfer, the recipient's willingness to pay is a dollar per dollar received. For a statistic, it is the money-valued gains and harms to everyone affected by the decisions the statistic changes, users or not, net of what the next-best source would have delivered, and someone has to model the decisions. Hayami and Peterson modeled grain storage and livestock output. John Abowd and Ian Schmutte frame a statistical agency's choice between privacy and accuracy as [operating where the marginal cost of privacy equals its marginal benefit](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.06303), and end their abstract on the missing input: "Further progress requires a better understanding of willingness-to-pay for privacy and statistical accuracy." Jeff Dominitz and Charles Manski [treat](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/88316/1/businessreview-2018-01-22-more-data-or-better-data-using-statistical.pdf) survey design itself as a decision problem, splitting a fixed budget between a cheap process with nonresponse and a costly one that measures accurately. The frameworks exist. The willingness-to-pay inputs they call for are mostly unmeasured.

**Allocation accuracy is mostly a transfer.** Bruce Spencer has worked on the benefit-cost analysis of data programs since 1980, and his loss function builds in the same asymmetry the UK benefit case counted. A constant, c, multiplies the total absolute misallocation. For 1970s revenue sharing he set c at 0.01: half the gap between the interest rate a local government pays when it is shorted and the rate it earns when it is overpaid. In 2017 he and his coauthors [applied](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10040110) the method to South Africa's decision on a 2016 census, which would have cost ZAR 3 billion more than the large household survey planned in its place. At c = 0.06, better grant allocations were worth ZAR 2.2 billion, about three quarters of that cost; above c = 0.08 they would have covered it. The government decided to skip the census and improve its post-censal estimates instead. The paper calls the choice of c "inherently subjective," and c values departures from the legislated formula; the authors judged the social welfare effects of misallocation "simply too difficult" to measure.

The American numbers are transfers too. Zachary Seeskin and Spencer [estimate](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/our-work/working-papers/2018/wp-18-10.html) that expected allocation errors across federal programs rise by $9 to $13 billion over a decade for each half point of root-mean-square error in state populations, holding programs and funding fixed. Andrew Reamer [simulated](https://gwipp.gwu.edu/counting-dollars-2020-estimating-fiscal-costs-census-undercount-states) an extra 1 percent net undercount in a single state's 2010 count and found it would have cost the state FY2015 resources in five programs whose federal share follows Medicaid's matching rate: a median of $1,091 per additional missed resident among the 35 states whose matching rate would stay above the formula's floor. A missed resident raises a state's measured income per person relative to the national figure, which can lower its federal share. Under a fixed-pot formula the misallocated dollars move between states; under Medicaid's match they move between a state and the federal Treasury. The dollars moved are not a welfare loss in themselves. The loss depends on who bears the change, on the weights placed on them, and on the taxes and services that adjust. Spencer's c and the UK benefit case's asymmetry are judgments that stand in for that calculation.

**Net fiscal effects and financing.** A statistic that changes what the government spends puts those changes in the denominator, and an MVPF above one settles nothing by itself. Hendren and Sprung-Keyser close the budget constraint by comparing a policy's MVPF with the MVPFs of other policies, so the value of a survey dollar depends on what would otherwise have been done with it.

Hayami and Peterson priced stated gains in survey accuracy in welfare terms. Spencer's South Africa analysis priced an additional census against a large survey and population estimates, with the value of more equitable allocations left open. The census valuations and the BLS estimate divide totals.

## Markets price the releases

A separate literature asks whether markets react to government data. They do. When the January 2019 government shutdown stopped USDA from publishing its outlook reports, Raghav Goyal and Michael Adjemian [estimated](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ags/aaea20/304256.html) from options data that an on-time report with a normal January impact would have made at-the-money corn options about 7 percent cheaper and soybean options about 31 percent cheaper. Abhiroop Mukherjee, George Panayotov and Janghoon Shon [used cloud cover](https://iems.ust.hk/assets/publications/working-papers-2019/iemswp2019-68.pdf) over the hubs commercial satellites watch to test whether satellite estimates substitute for government data, and found that for some series "markets are no longer surprised by government announcements." Thomas Gilbert, Chiara Scotti, Georg Strasser and Clara Vega [find](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econresdata/feds/2015/files/2015046r1pap.pdf) that a release's ability to nowcast GDP, inflation and the policy rate explains a significant share of how far Treasury yields move on it.

A price reaction shows the release carried information. It does not price the decisions that change because of it.

## The MVPF paper values better evidence

Section VI.A of the Hendren and Sprung-Keyser paper is titled "Value of information in evidence-based policy making." For each policy, they compute the share of a dollar a policymaker would pay to remove the sampling uncertainty in the policy's estimated effects before spending it. The answer is 50 cents for the introduction of food stamps and zero for the effect of a more lenient disability-insurance judge, whose impact is already precisely estimated. The highest values fall on policies with uncertain long-run effects on children.

Then they value a gain in precision from a dataset. Economists first estimated the long-run effects of food stamps on children from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics, a federally supported survey, and later from linked Census data. With the survey estimates, beneficiaries' willingness to pay per dollar of program cost comes out at 6.06, with a 95 percent confidence interval from −12.07 to 23.78. With the linked data, it comes out at 1.09, from −2.45 to 4.55. Under their prior and financing assumptions, a policymaker holding only the survey estimate would pay 24 cents per dollar of food stamp spending to decide with the linked data's precision instead. The authors call it "a stylized exercise." It is a value of better evidence, derived inside the MVPF framework and priced per dollar of the program the evidence informs. It is not an MVPF of the data: the denominator is food stamp spending, and the cost of producing the data never enters.

In this incomplete search, I have not found an MVPF estimate for a statistical program's marginal spending across its many uses. The Current Population Survey feeds the [official poverty statistics](https://www.census.gov/topics/income-poverty/poverty/about.html) and the microsimulation models, [PolicyEngine's](https://policyengine.github.io/policyengine-us-data/data/) among them, that estimate who gains and loses from a proposed tax or benefit change. A larger sample can improve precision across many of those estimates, and its value depends on which decisions the precision would change. Some, like the disability judge, would pay nothing. What would that dollar buy?

The studies above, and about forty more, are in [an annotated bibliography](/blog/marginal-value-of-information-bibliography/) that records how far I checked each number.
