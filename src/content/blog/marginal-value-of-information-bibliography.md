A companion to [Is the marginal value of information an MVPF?](/blog/marginal-value-of-information/), compiled September 2026. Every entry carries its verification state:

- **[text]** Checked against the source's full text or official abstract.
- **[checker]** An automated citation check confirmed it against the source.
- **[meta]** Crossref confirms the item exists with these authors, year and venue. Numbers not checked.
- **[unread]** Found in a search and not yet checked. Treat as a lead.

## 1. Marginal value of accuracy, decision-theoretic

- **Hayami and Peterson (1970, revised 1971).** Social returns to public information services: the case of statistical reporting of U.S. farm commodities. University of Minnesota Staff Paper P70-14. doi:10.22004/ag.econ.13711. Published as Hayami and Peterson (1972), *American Economic Review* 62: 119-130. **[venue confirmed by citation, article unread]** I read that reference in Isengildina Massa, Karali and Irwin's review, which also calls it "one of the only studies that compared the costs of data collection to an estimate of the benefits." Crossref has no record of the article. The figures below are from the staff paper; I have not checked that the AER version reports the same ones. Welfare loss from sampling error in USDA production statistics. Food and feed grains get an inventory adjustment model, where holders misjudge stocks; livestock get a production adjustment model, where producers misjudge output. Each commodity gets one model, so the authors call the estimates lower bounds. **[text, from the staff paper]** Each extra dollar spent cutting typical sampling error from 2.5 to 2.0 percent "returns more than 600 dollars"; from 2.0 to 1.5 percent, "90 to 100 dollars". Authors conclude there is underinvestment. Staff paper: https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/13711
- **Isengildina Massa, Karali and Irwin (2023).** What do we know about the value and market impact of the US Department of Agriculture reports? *Applied Economic Perspectives and Policy* 46: 698-736. doi:10.1002/aepp.13409. **[meta for the citation; text for the content, from the June 2023 manuscript at https://scotthirwin.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/USDA_Impact_06_2023.pdf]** The survey of this strand. "Only a handful of studies provide direct estimates of the welfare benefits of USDA information", using rational expectations models of storable commodity markets; most work instead infers value from market reactions to releases. Its summary judgment: the direct studies "suggest the social welfare value of public situation and outlook information substantially exceeds the cost."
- **Bradford and Kelejian (1977, 1978).** The value of information for crop forecasting in a market system: some theoretical issues, *Review of Economic Studies*, doi:10.2307/2296905; and ... with Bayesian speculators: theory and empirical results, *Bell Journal of Economics*, doi:10.2307/3003616. **[meta]** The $64 million (1975 dollars) net social loss from imperfect wheat forecasts is **[second-hand, as reported by the review above]**.
- **Freebairn (1976).** The value and distribution of the benefits of commodity price outlook information. *Economic Record* 52(2). doi:10.1111/j.1475-4932.1976.tb01578.x. **[meta]** Australian counterpart to the strand. Unread.
- **Spencer (1980).** Benefit-cost analysis of data used to allocate funds. Springer. **[text, as described in Spencer et al. 2017]** Loss is c times the sum of absolute misallocations. Spencer set c = 0.01 for 1970s General Revenue Sharing, derived as half the gap between local governments' borrowing and investing rates.
- **Spencer, May, Kenyon and Seeskin (2017).** Cost-benefit analysis for a quinquennial census: the 2016 population census of South Africa. *Journal of Official Statistics* 33(1): 249-274. doi:10.1515/JOS-2017-0013. **[text]** Census cost at least ZAR 3 billion. Expected reduction in misallocation ZAR 4.8 billion for one grant and ZAR 32.1 billion for another, before scaling by c: the first for the provincial equitable share, the second for the local government equitable share. At c = 0.06 the allocation benefit is ZAR 2.2 billion, about three quarters of the ZAR 3 billion extra cost over a community survey; above c = 0.08 it covers the cost. Benefits cover 2017-2021 in 2011 prices. The constant c values departures from the legislated formula; the authors judged social welfare effects of misallocation "simply too difficult" to measure. The government decided not to run the census. The paper names Redfern (1974) as the earliest such analysis.
- **Seeskin and Spencer (2018).** Balancing 2020 census cost and accuracy. Northwestern IPR WP-18-10. **[text]** "Expected distortions in fund allocations increase about $9-$13 billion for each 0.5% increase in average error." State error in 2010 averaged 0.6% RMS. The body puts expected misallocation at $80 billion for the decade at 4% RMS error, rising linearly, so the per-half-point figure is over the decade.
- **Seeskin and Spencer (2015).** Effects of census accuracy on apportionment of Congress and allocations of federal funds. IPR WP-15-05. **[text, read 2026-09-18; the retained download is a 404 page]** Under the uncorrelated-errors model, at 1% relative RMSE, 3.4 expected malapportioned House seats, with a 1 in 6 chance of 6 or more. At 4%, a 3 in 4 chance of 12 or more. The 2018 working paper's appendix reproduces these: 3.38 expected seats, and probabilities of 0.168 and 0.752.
- **Spencer (1985).** Optimal data quality. *JASA* 80(391). doi:10.2307/2288471. **[meta]**
- **Dominitz and Manski (2017).** More data or better data? A statistical decision problem. *Review of Economic Studies* 84(4). doi:10.1093/restud/rdx005. **[text, abstract only]** Uses Wald statistical decision theory to allocate a fixed budget between a cheap sampling process with nonresponse or interval-coded answers and a costly one that measures accurately, choosing the minimax-regret design. The abstract states no headline number. A sample-size threshold sometimes attributed to this paper does not appear in the abstract and is left out.
- **Elbers, Fujii, Lanjouw, Ozler and Yin (2007).** Poverty alleviation through geographic targeting: how much does disaggregation help? *Journal of Development Economics* 83(1); World Bank WPS 3419. **[text, from the working paper]** Finer poverty maps reach a uniform transfer's poverty reduction at 58% of the cost in rural Ecuador (83% with coarse regions), 37% in Madagascar, 31% in Cambodia.

## 2. Accuracy versus privacy as a welfare problem

- **Abowd and Schmutte (2019).** An economic analysis of privacy protection and statistical accuracy as social choices. *AER* 109(1): 171-202. **[text, arXiv 1808.06303]** "Operate where the marginal cost of increasing privacy equals the marginal benefit." Title I application with about 13,000 districts and 46 million children. The abstract says progress needs better estimates of willingness to pay for accuracy.
- **Hsu et al. (2014)**, doi:10.1109/CSF.2014.35; **Kazan and Reiter (2024)**, NeurIPS; **Su, Su and Wang (2025)**, PNAS; **Petti and Flaxman (2020)**, Gates Open Research. **[checker: supported]**
- **Hawes et al. (2025)**, arXiv 2502.07105; **Jarmin et al. (2023)**, PNAS. **[checker: partially supported]**
- **Steed, Liu, Wu and Acquisti (2022).** Policy impacts of statistical uncertainty and privacy. *Science* 377. doi:10.1126/science.abq4481. **[meta]** Dollar figures are behind the paywall and unread.
- **Hotz et al. (2022)** PNAS; **Kenny et al. (2021)** Science Advances; **Ruggles et al. (2019)** and **Abowd et al. (2019)** AEA P&P. **[meta]**

## 3. Whole-program benefit-cost valuations

- **Bloom, Groshen, Hobbs and Strain (2026).** The value of reliable statistics. NBER WP 35135. **[text]** Event study of the August 2025 firing of the BLS commissioner. Implied output loss "roughly $20 billion" against a FY2025 BLS budget of $704 million, so "about $25 for every $1". Their Table 3 gives $19.30 billion, and $19.30 to $20 billion over $704 million is $27 to $28, so the authors' "$25" is below the ratio their own figures imply. The authors call it a back-of-the-envelope estimate of "the marginal value of public trust in official statistics".
- **Bakker (2014), Valuing the census**, Statistics New Zealand. **[text]** NPV "close to $1 billion" over 25 years at 8%, "every dollar invested in the census generates a net benefit of five dollars." The 2021 refresh restates this valuation as "$6 of benefits per dollar spent." **Bakker (2021)** refresh. **[text]** Benefits about NZ$2.8 billion on costs of about NZ$640 million, around $4 per dollar.
- **Lateral Economics (2019), Valuing the Australian census.** **[text]** A$666 million gross annual value, over A$800 million with a 25% long-tail uplift, against A$670 million per five years. About $6 per $1.
- **ONS, Assessing the benefits of Census 2021, England and Wales (2022 article; methods page; UKSA paper EAP185, 2023).** **[text]** The Census 2021 business case forecast "around £5 of benefit in the wider economy for every £1 spent" and £5.5 billion over ten years. For the later assessment of realized benefits, ONS assumes the net welfare gain from census-informed funding is 0.015% of the amount allocated for population estimates and 0.053% for attribute data, carried over from departments' earlier modelling of next-best-data misallocation. EAP185 also asks its review panel whether to add a 25% "long tail" uplift as the Australian valuation did.
- **Cope (2015), The value of census statistics in England and Wales**, ISI World Statistics Congress. **[text]** For the 2011 census, the health allocation formula was rerun on GP patient lists, the next-best data, for a first-year error of £800m, and tested local government allocations for £125m. The pots are fixed, so gains and losses cancel; ONS counted the net efficiency loss from the asymmetry between surplus and shortfall, a modeled £70m a year for these allocations together, decaying as the data aged.
- **Deloitte (2016), Measuring the value of statistics**, for ONS. **[text]** Core user benefits of GDP, inflation and labour market statistics worth £130-155 million for 2014, benefit-cost ratio about 1.7:1 on £77.1 million. Excludes wider benefits; a ready-reckoner estimate including them gives about £0.5 billion and a ratio of 6.7, which the authors urge interpreting "with caution."

## 4. Dollars moved by statistical error

- **Reamer (2018), Counting for dollars 2020, report 2.** **[text]** Covers five programs guided by the Federal Medical Assistance Percentage (FMAP): Medicaid, CHIP, Title IV-E Foster Care and Adoption Assistance, and the Child Care and Development Fund, $286.1 billion in FY2015. A simulation: an additional 1% net undercount in one state's 2010 count, other states unchanged, raises that state's per capita income relative to the nation and lowers its FMAP, cutting federal reimbursements and raising state payments. Reamer reports a median of $1,091 (Tennessee), from $533 (Utah) to $2,309 (Vermont), and calls these the 37 states above the 50% floor. His appendix labels the median as covering states that stay above the floor, which is 35 of them: Illinois ($953) and Washington ($39) would hit the floor. Across all 37 states with losses, the median is $1,020 (Kansas). The dollars moved are not themselves a measure of welfare loss.
- **Duggan and Gillingham (1999)**, *JBES* 17(2), doi:10.1080/07350015.1999.10524806. Existence confirmed. Its $26 billion overpayment figure is **[unread]**.
- **Murray (1992)**, *Demography*; Boskin Commission (1996); GAO (2000); CBO (2013). **[unread]**

## 5. Revealed value: how markets price government data releases

Indirect evidence. A price reaction proves the release carried information; it does not by itself measure welfare.

- **Gilbert, Scotti, Strasser and Vega (2017).** Is the intrinsic value of a macroeconomic news announcement related to its asset price impact? *Journal of Monetary Economics* 92: 78-95. doi:10.1016/j.jmoneco.2017.09.008. **[meta; text, abstract of the FEDS version]** Defines an announcement's intrinsic value as its ability to nowcast GDP, inflation and the funds rate, and finds it explains "a significant fraction" of variation in Treasury yield reactions. Timeliness and relation to fundamentals matter most.
- **Rigobon and Sack (2006).** Noisy macroeconomic announcements, monetary policy, and asset prices. NBER WP 12420; chapter in *Asset Prices and Monetary Policy* (2008), pp. 335-369. **[meta; text, abstract]** Correcting for noise in measured surprises, asset prices are "much more responsive to incoming news than previously believed."
- **Goyal and Adjemian (2021).** The 2019 government shutdown increased uncertainty in major agricultural commodity markets. *Food Policy* 102: 102064. doi:10.1016/j.foodpol.2021.102064. **[meta; text, abstract of the 2020 AAEA version]** A natural experiment in withholding data. Had the January 2019 USDA report appeared on time with a normal January report's impact, at-the-money corn options would have been 7% cheaper (6.1 to 8.2%) and soybean options 31% cheaper (24 to 35%). If it had instead matched the volatility drop at the February makeup release, 22% and 43%.
- **Mukherjee, Panayotov and Shon (2021).** Eye in the sky: private satellites and government macro data. *Journal of Financial Economics* 141: 234-254. doi:10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.03.002. **[meta; text, abstract of the 2019 working paper]** Uses cloud cover to identify the effect of commercial satellite estimates on the price impact of government releases; for some series "markets are no longer surprised by government announcements." The value of a public dataset depends on the next-best substitute.
- **Arnade, Hoffman and Effland (2021).** The impact of public information on commodity market performance: the response of corn futures to USDA corn production forecasts. USDA ERS Economic Research Report 293. **[text, summary]** USDA's own study: corn production projections move corn futures even controlling for other WASDE variables, most strongly in August and September.
- **Cady and Pellechio (2006).** Sovereign borrowing cost and the IMF's data standards initiatives. IMF WP 06/78. doi:10.5089/9781451863383.001. **[text, abstract]** Across 26 emerging and developing countries, subscribing to the Special Data Dissemination Standard cuts launch spreads by an average of 20 percent; the General Data Dissemination System by 8 percent for countries with market access.
- **Glennerster and Shin (2003, 2008).** Is transparency good for you, and can the IMF help? IMF WP 03/132, doi:10.5089/9781451855401.001; published as Does transparency pay? *IMF Staff Papers* 55: 183-209. **[meta; text, Crossref abstract]** Sovereign spreads fall after countries adopt IMF transparency reforms, with effects "economically important" for low-initial-transparency countries.

## 5b. Developing countries

- **Dalberg, Investment case: multiplying progress through data ecosystems** (Data With Purpose, with the Global Partnership for Sustainable Development Data). **[text; advocacy document, methodology unread]** Claims "an average return of USD 32 for every dollar" invested in data, range USD 7 to USD 73, from its own analysis of past investments.
- **Hanna and Olken.** Universal basic incomes vs. targeted transfers. NBER WP 24939. **[text, abstract]** In Indonesia and Peru, proxy-means-tested targeting yields "substantially higher welfare gains" than universal transfers for a given budget. Tangential: it values targeting, and only implicitly the data behind it.
- **Beuermann, Hoffmann, Stampini and Vargas (2025).** Shooting a moving target: evaluating targeting tools for social programs when income fluctuates. *Journal of Development Economics* 172: 103395. **[meta]** Unread.

## 6. Neighbouring literature with transferable methods

- **Molina and Rudik (2024)**, NBER WP 32548. **[text]** Hurricane forecast gains since 2007 cut total costs 23%, about $2 billion per hurricane.
- **Shrader, Bakkensen and Lemoine (2023)**, NBER WP 31361. **[text, NBER abstract]** Forecasts 50% more accurate would save 2,200 lives a year. Willingness to pay $112 billion for that improvement over the remainder of the century, $22 billion of it from facilitated climate adaptation.
- **Straub, Koontz and Loomis (2019)**, USGS OFR 2019-1112. **[text]** Landsat benefits $3.45 billion in 2017, up from $2.19 billion in 2011. **Loomis, Koontz and Ravichandran (2024).** **[text]** $25.63 billion to registered users in 2023.
- **Loomis et al. (2015)**, **Bernknopf et al. (2018)**, **Cooke et al. (2013)**, Value of information for climate observing systems. **[meta]**
- Lazo et al. (2009), RTI GPS report (2019), Nagaraj (2022), Katz and Murphy (1997). **[unread]**

## Frameworks for deciding how much to spend on data

- **Hendren and Sprung-Keyser (2020).** A unified welfare analysis of government policies. *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 135(3): 1209-1318. doi:10.1093/qje/qjaa006. Section VI.A, "Value of information in evidence-based policy making". **[meta; text, checked against the published QJE article]** The closest thing I found to an MVPF-linked value of information. Because the MVPF is a price, "a welfare-maximizing government should be willing to pay to reduce the uncertainty in these prices." They value reduced sampling uncertainty in each policy's MVPF estimate and plot it for every policy (Figure VIII), motivated by "improved access to larger administrative longitudinal data sets." Magnitudes: the highest values are for "policies with uncertain long-run effects on children"; $0.50 per dollar of spending for the introduction of food stamps; $0 for the effect of a more lenient disability-insurance judge, whose impact is already precisely estimated. They "defer an exhaustive treatment to future work." Subsection VI.A.2, on administrative versus survey data for the long-run impacts of food stamps, values a hypothetical gain in precision from a dataset: with PSID survey estimates the beneficiaries' willingness to pay per dollar is 6.06 (95% CI −12.07 to 23.78); with linked Census data 1.09 (−2.45 to 4.55); a policymaker holding the PSID estimate would pay $0.24 per dollar of food stamp spending to decide with the Census data's precision. The authors call it "a stylized exercise": it assumes an uninformed prior and financing that targets the same beneficiaries. The denominator is food stamp spending, and the cost of producing the data never enters, so this is a value of better evidence derived within the MVPF framework, not an MVPF of the data. Free copy of the published article, hosted by the coauthor: https://bsprungkeyser.com/assets/MVPF_Mar_2020_QJE.pdf. The February 2020 working version (https://opportunityinsights.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Welfare_paper.pdf) and the August 2019 NBER working paper report the same 24 cents.
- **Bank of England, Cost-benefit analysis of monetary and financial statistics: a practical guide** (main author Andrew Holder). **[text, opening pages only]** A handbook on how the Bank applied cost-benefit analysis to its own statistics. Undated in what I read; it mentions a July 2005 workshop.

## What is not covered

1. **Market reactions to data releases.** Partly covered in section 5. The individual USDA-report studies that the Isengildina Massa, Karali and Irwin review organises are not checked here.
2. **Developing countries.** Partly covered in section 5b, through the IMF transparency papers and one advocacy document.
3. **An MVPF-style ratio for statistical spending.** Hendren and Sprung-Keyser value a hypothetical gain in precision from one dataset for one policy (above), per dollar of that policy's spending. I found no MVPF estimate for a statistical program's marginal spending across the many decisions its data inform. The search behind that negative was incomplete.

About 100 further candidates from the initial search were never checked and are not listed.
